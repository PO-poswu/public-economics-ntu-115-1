Ramsey: linear demand

Let \(\frac{1}{k} = 1 + k'\) so that \(\frac{Q_j}{b_j t_j} = \frac{1}{k}\), or \(\frac{b_j}{Q_j} t_j = k\).

\[\eta^d_j = - \frac{dQ/Q}{dP/P} = - \frac{dQ}{dP} \frac{P}{Q} = - \left( - b_j \right) \frac{p + t}{Q} = \frac{b_j}{Q} \left( p + t \right)\]

\(\frac{\eta^d_j}{p + t_j} = \frac{b_j}{Q_j} \Rightarrow \eta^d_j \frac{t_j}{p + t_j} = k \Rightarrow \frac{t_j}{p + t_j} = \frac{k}{\eta^d_j}\)

Taxes are inversely proportional to demand elasticities: \[\frac{t_j \color{red} \downarrow}{p + t_j \color{red} \downarrow} = \frac{k}{\eta^d_j \color{red} \uparrow}\]

Ramsey taxes

  • The tax rates should be set so that the increase in deadweight loss per extra dollar raised is the same for each commodity.
  • Commodities with low elasticity of demand (or low elasticity of supply) have a lower marginal deadweight loss per marginal dollar of revenue raised \(\Rightarrow\) should face higher marginal tax rates

Income tax was fairly recent

Income tax history

DURING the 1930s, no more than five percent of Americans were income taxpayers. The tax was viewed as a “class tax” directed toward the rich–those President Roosevelt referred to as “economic royalists.” The individual income tax accounted for only between ten and twenty percent of federal revenues during the 1930s. World War II dramatically altered this very limited conception of the income tax. Federal budget expenditures increased more than twelve times from 1940 to 1945. Shortages of goods and increased consumer purchasing power produced a worrisome inflationary situation. For government officials, the income tax came to be seen as both a war financing device and as a means of decreasing excess purchasing power. The result was that the income tax rolls increased from about 7 million taxpayers in 1940 to more than 42 million in 1945. The income tax became in Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau, Jr.’s words, “a people’s tax.”

Jones, Carolyn C. “Class Tax to Mass Tax: The Role of Propaganda in the Expansion of the Income Tax during World War II.” Buffalo Law Review 37, no. 3 (1988): 685-738.

Orient Your Audience From the Start

  1. What is my level of expertise relative to my audience?
  2. How do I want to change my readers’ view of my topic?
  3. How much does my audience already know about the problem/issue I am addressing? How much do they care about it?
  4. What’s the “news” in my paper? What constitutes old information and new information for my audience?
  5. How resistant is my audience to my thesis?
  6. How busy is my audience?
